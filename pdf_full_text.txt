Risk-Sensitive Stochastic Model Predictive Control (SMPC) for Autonomous Rover
Navigation in High-Variance Terrain
Team TerraRisk
Sivaganesh B. • Sushil Krishna K. • Ramya Rasika S. R. • Kanishka S.
Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur
August 15, 2026

Abstract
Autonomous rovers operating on unstructured, deformable terrain,planetary regolith, sand, or mud,must plan
trajectories under substantial and often heavy-tailed uncertainty in soil mechanics. Standard deterministic Model
Predictive Control (MPC) and Gaussian Stochastic MPC (SMPC) either react too conservatively to unknown
terrain or under-weight rare but catastrophic entrapment events caused by hidden pockets of highly
compressible soil. Existing risk-aware navigation methods largely infer this risk from exteroceptive,
geometry-based sensing (LiDAR and camera imagery of elevation, roughness, or slope), which cannot distinguish
visually similar terrain patches that differ sharply in underlying soil strength. This project formulates a
Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)-based SMPC controller that supplements standard LiDAR/camera perception
with direct physical measurements of the rover's own interaction with the ground,wheel slip, sinkage depth,
drive-motor torque, and body attitude,feeding a Bekker-Wong terramechanics model so that risk is evaluated
from the soil's actual mechanical behaviour rather than its appearance alone. The controller is benchmarked in
simulation against deterministic NMPC and Gaussian SMPC baselines on entrapment rate and path efficiency.

1. Engineering System
The engineering system under study is a class of Off-Road Unmanned Ground Vehicles (UGVs), with particular
emphasis on planetary exploration rovers. The scope is narrowed to the independent wheel-drive and steering
control architecture of a skid-steer or Ackermann-type rover operating on unstructured, deformable ground
where the vehicle-terrain interaction model,captured through classical Bekker-Wong terramechanics,is only
known probabilistically. The controller sits at the trajectory-planning and low-level actuation interface: it
consumes probabilistic terrain estimates and produces steering-angle and wheel-torque/velocity commands over
a finite receding horizon. Unlike prior systems that derive these terrain estimates purely from exteroceptive
sensing (LiDAR/camera), the rover here is additionally instrumented to sense its own physical interaction with
the ground, as detailed in Section 3.

2. Problem Statement
In off-road and extraterrestrial navigation, terrain information is typically available only as probabilistic estimates
of soil cohesion and internal friction angle, obtained from orbital imagery or forward-facing sensors. Classical
MPC and even standard Gaussian SMPC formulations assume light-tailed (typically Gaussian) uncertainty and
enforce simple chance constraints on state variables. Real terrain hazards, however,such as isolated pockets of

highly compressible soil hidden beneath a visually uniform surface,exhibit heavy-tailed statistics: rare but severe
events dominate risk.
A further, related limitation runs through most existing risk-aware off-road planners, including the closest prior
work identified in Section 4.3: their risk estimate is built almost entirely from LiDAR and camera data,that is,
from the geometry and appearance of the terrain (elevation, roughness, slope). These sensors answer “what
does the ground look like,” not “how will the ground actually behave under my wheel.” Two patches of terrain
can be geometrically and visually near-identical,flat, uniform sand,while one is firmly packed and fully traversable
and the other is loosely packed and capable of causing entrapment. A purely LiDAR/camera-based risk map
cannot tell these apart, which forces a choice between two failure modes: treat all visually-ambiguous terrain as
safe (under-reacts to real tail risk) or treat all of it as hazardous (over-reacts, discards otherwise-traversable
routes, and needlessly restricts the paths the rover is willing to explore).
When a rover enters an undetected hazard zone, excessive wheel slip drives sinkage past a recoverable
threshold, resulting in irreversible entrapment and mission loss. The central control-theoretic problem addressed
by this project is therefore twofold: (i) to ground terrain-risk estimation in the physical soil-mechanical quantities
that actually govern sinkage, rather than in geometric/visual proxies alone, and (ii) to design a
trajectory-planning and control law that explicitly bounds the worst-case (tail) risk of catastrophic sinkage arising
from that estimate, rather than only its expected value,while remaining computationally tractable within a
receding-horizon framework and respecting hard actuator limits such as motor torque bounds.

3. Objectives
•​ Develop a stochastic vehicle-terrain model: formulate a simplified rover dynamic model with probabilistic

wheel-slip and sinkage states, parameterised by uncertain soil cohesion and internal friction angle
following Bekker-Wong terramechanics.
•​ Extend terrain sensing beyond LiDAR/camera: in addition to standard exteroceptive perception (used for

geometry, obstacles, and coarse terrain classification), incorporate direct physical/proprioceptive
measurements,wheel slip ratio (commanded vs. actual wheel-ground speed), wheel sinkage depth
(suspension deflection or visual wheel-track depth), drive-motor torque/current, and IMU-sensed body
pitch/roll,so that soil cohesion, friction angle, and sinkage exponent can be inferred from the rover's actual
physical interaction with the ground, not only from its visual appearance.
•​ Formulate a coherent risk measure: integrate Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) into the MPC cost function

to penalise the expected value of worst-case sinkage outcomes rather than only their mean, using the
physically-grounded terrain estimate above as its input rather than a geometry-only risk map.
•​ Design the CVaR-SMPC controller: construct a risk-sensitive stochastic MPC that jointly optimises steering

and velocity/torque commands over a finite prediction horizon, subject to hard actuator constraints (motor
torque limits) and a tunable risk-bounded chance constraint on catastrophic sinkage probability.
•​ Benchmark in simulation: evaluate entrapment rate versus path efficiency for the proposed CVaR-SMPC

against (i) a deterministic Nonlinear MPC (NMPC) baseline and (ii) a Gaussian-noise SMPC baseline, on a
stochastically generated terrain map with patchy high-risk zones, and specifically test whether the added

physical sensing allows the rover to safely traverse and explore more of the visually-ambiguous terrain than
a LiDAR/camera-only baseline, without increasing entrapment rate.

4. Literature Survey
The proposal draws on three intersecting bodies of work: predictive control for rover and off-road trajectory
tracking, terramechanics-based slip-sinkage modelling, and CVaR-based risk-sensitive planning and control.
Representative works and the corresponding gap this project targets are summarised below.

4.1 Predictive Control for Rover / Off-Road Trajectory Tracking
Zheng and Zhu [1] developed a stochastic time-varying MPC for trajectory tracking of a wheeled mobile robot,
showing that explicitly modelling time-varying uncertainty in the tracking-error dynamics improves robustness
over a fixed-noise-model MPC. Li, Du, and Zhang [2] surveyed path-planning approaches for autonomous
vehicles specifically in off-road scenarios, highlighting that most off-road planners still rely on geometric or
cost-map heuristics,i.e., LiDAR/camera-derived elevation and roughness,rather than a probabilistic,
physically-grounded terrain model embedded in the controller itself. He, Sun, Yang, and Gao [3] designed an
MPC-based trajectory-tracking controller for a novel wheeled-legged planetary rover, demonstrating that MPC is
a practical on-board control architecture for rovers with complex mobility mechanisms, but under a deterministic
terrain assumption. Taken together, this body of work establishes MPC as the standard trajectory-tracking
framework for wheeled and rover platforms, yet none of these three formulations treats the terrain interaction
itself as an uncertain, risk-bearing quantity inside the optimization, and none incorporates physical
(proprioceptive) sensing of soil behaviour.

4.2 Terramechanics-Based Slip-Sinkage Modelling
Ishigami, Miwa, Nagatani, and Yoshida [4] derived a terramechanics-based steering model for planetary rovers
on loose soil, extending Bekker-Wong theory to predict slip and lateral force during steering manoeuvres. Ding,
Gao, Deng, and Tao [5] developed a wheel slip-sinkage prediction model for lunar rovers, refining the sinkage
exponent to capture how sinkage grows disproportionately with slip ratio rather than remaining a static function
of vertical load. Both works provide accurate, physically grounded slip-sinkage models built from exactly the kind
of physical quantities (slip, torque, sinkage) this project proposes to sense onboard,but both are used for state
estimation and reactive traction control rather than being embedded as the uncertain dynamics inside a
predictive planner: the model informs the controller only after slip has begun, not before the rover commits to a
hazardous region, and neither work couples this physical sensing with a forward-looking, risk-sensitive
optimization.

4.3 CVaR and Risk-Sensitive Predictive Control
Blackmore, Ono, and Williams [6] formulated chance-constrained optimal path planning with obstacles,
establishing the standard probabilistic-constraint baseline against which CVaR-based methods are typically
compared; chance constraints bound only the probability of a violation, not its severity, so they cannot
distinguish a barely-exceeded constraint from a catastrophic one. Chow, Tamar, Mannor, and Pavone [7] provided
the foundational CVaR-based framework for risk-sensitive and robust sequential decision-making, establishing
CVaR as a coherent risk measure that can be optimized via convex reformulation,the theoretical basis this project

builds its cost formulation on. Wang, Schuurmans, and Patrinos [8] proposed a sequential convexification
algorithm for risk-sensitive, interaction-aware MPC with decision-dependent uncertainty, showing that a
nonconvex risk-sensitive MPC problem can be solved efficiently as a sequence of convex problems,directly
relevant to keeping a CVaR-SMPC solvable in real time on rover-class hardware.
Closest to this proposal, Moon, Seong, Shim, Kim, Cho, and Lee [9] proposed a sampling-based MPC with
CVaR-based risk assessment for off-road environments, building a recursive CVaR risk map from elevation data
and demonstrating improved success rate and smoother motion over hybrid A* and standard MPC baselines on
real ground-robot trials. This is the nearest prior work to the present proposal: it confirms that CVaR-weighted
risk maps meaningfully improve off-road MPC safety. However,consistent with the sensing gap identified in
Section 2,its risk map is built from terrain elevation data alone (i.e., LiDAR/camera geometry), and it does not
model the underlying slip-sinkage dynamics that actually cause entrapment, nor does it draw on any
physical/proprioceptive measurement of the rover's own wheel-soil interaction. It uses CVaR to reshape the
sampling distribution of a path-integral planner from geometric risk, rather than to constrain a
terramechanics-based dynamic model informed by physical sensing.
The specific gap this project targets is therefore twofold: first, to replace or supplement LiDAR/camera-only,
geometry-based risk estimation with direct physical measurement of wheel slip, sinkage, torque, and attitude,
feeding a Bekker-Wong stochastic slip-sinkage model; and second, to couple that physically-grounded model
directly with a CVaR-constrained SMPC optimization, so that the tail-risk of catastrophic sinkage,evaluated from
actual soil-mechanical behaviour rather than surface appearance,is what shapes the rover's trajectory. This is
expected to allow the controller to safely traverse and explore terrain that a geometry-only risk map would
conservatively avoid, while remaining appropriately cautious in genuinely hazardous zones.

5. Proposed Approach: From LiDAR/Camera to Physically-Grounded Risk (Initial Solution)
The starting point for the controller is standard exteroceptive perception,LiDAR and camera data,used, as in prior
work, for obstacle geometry and a coarse, appearance-based terrain classification (e.g., “sand,” “gravel,” “rock”).
This alone, however, only estimates what the terrain looks like, not how it will mechanically behave under load.
To close that gap, the following physical quantities are proposed as additional inputs, each already accessible
from standard rover instrumentation or directly readable from the simulation environment:
•​ Wheel slip ratio,computed by comparing commanded wheel angular velocity against actual ground speed

(from visual odometry or wheel encoders); large, persistent mismatch indicates loss of traction consistent
with soft or loose soil.
•​ Wheel sinkage depth,measured via suspension deflection, or, in simulation, read directly from the physics

engine's wheel-ground contact state.
•​ Drive-motor torque / current,elevated torque at low forward speed indicates the wheel is working against

soil resistance rather than rolling freely, a classic terramechanics signature of sinkage in progress.
•​ IMU-sensed body pitch, roll, and vibration,detects asymmetric sinkage (e.g., one side of the rover sinking

faster than the other) before it becomes visually obvious.

These four signals are precisely the quantities that the Bekker-Wong equations relate to soil cohesion, internal
friction angle, and sinkage exponent: cohesion and friction angle physically cause a given slip/sinkage/torque
response, so observing the response allows the soil parameters,and hence the risk of further, worse sinkage,to
be inferred, rather than merely guessed from appearance.
Given the course timeline, the physical-sensing pipeline is proposed as an incremental, three-stage build,
matching the project's three scheduled presentations:
•​ Stage 1 (for the first presentation): known/assumed soil parameters are assigned per terrain patch from

published terramechanics tables, to validate the CVaR-SMPC formulation and controller in isolation from
the sensing problem.
•​ Stage 2: coarse LiDAR/camera-based terrain classification is added, mapping each visual class to a

parameter range (with uncertainty) drawn from the literature, so the controller must reason about
imperfect, appearance-only knowledge,reproducing the limitation of prior work such as [9] as an explicit
baseline.
•​ Stage 3: the physical/proprioceptive signals above are added and fused with a recursive online estimator,

so that soil parameters,and the resulting CVaR-bounded risk,are updated in situ as the rover drives, rather
than assumed or classified from appearance alone.
This staged approach directly supports the benchmarking objective in Section 3: at Stage 2, the controller is
effectively equivalent in sensing to prior LiDAR/camera-only risk-aware planners, providing a fair baseline; at
Stage 3, the added physical sensing is what should allow the rover to distinguish genuinely hazardous terrain
from merely visually-ambiguous terrain, and therefore travel and explore more of the map safely rather than
defaulting to conservative avoidance.

6. Expected Outcomes
•​ A mathematical formulation of the CVaR-SMPC controller specifically tailored to terramechanics-based

slip-sinkage dynamics.
•​ A functional software simulation (Python/MATLAB) demonstrating rover navigation over a stochastically

generated terrain map with patchy high-risk zones.
•​ A working online estimation pipeline that infers Bekker-Wong soil parameters from wheel slip, sinkage,

torque, and IMU data, in addition to the baseline LiDAR/camera terrain classification.
•​ Quantitative comparison of entrapment rate and mission-completion time against deterministic NMPC and

Gaussian SMPC baselines, and specifically against a LiDAR/camera-only risk-map baseline (Stage 2), to
isolate the benefit of the added physical sensing.
•​ Visual analysis (trajectory maps / phase plots) showing that the prediction horizon actively routes the rover

around zones of high tail-risk,and, where physical sensing confirms terrain is safer than it appears, through
visually-ambiguous zones that a geometry-only planner would have avoided.
